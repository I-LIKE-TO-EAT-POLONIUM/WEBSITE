History
Etymology
  • The country's name derives from "Ălgrø," the name of the clan credited with the island's first settlement, combined with "-vík," a native word meaning "bay" — together meaning roughly "Bay of the Ălgrø"
  • Spelling convention: "Algrovik" is the English spelling, used in ordinary reading and everyday text. The native spelling "Ălgrøvík" is used for government, department, and other official names and uses (for example, the Ălgrøvík Transit Authority and the Kingdom of Ălgrøvík)
Kingdom of Ălgrøvík (KOĂ)
  • Founded at the original settlement of the island by the Ălgrø clan, in what is now Åbóvìk, Østháv — the country's oldest city
  • Flag: View Flag
Empire of Ălgrøvík (EOĂ)
  • Formed roughly 31 years after the KOĂ, on the opposite side of the island in what is now Skjærmørk, by a splinter group of the Ălgrø clan who left the original settlement in search of better resources and established their own governing body
  • The EOĂ's side of the island had significantly more abundant resources and better living conditions than the KOĂ's side — a key factor behind the splinter group's original departure, and later a major advantage during the Civil War
  • Civil war broke out about 1 year after the EOĂ's founding — a conflict between two branches of the same founding clan rather than between separate peoples
  • The EOĂ won, defeating the KOĂ and unifying the island — its stronger resource base and living conditions were a significant factor in the outcome
  • Flag: View Flag

The modern federal capital, Víkí, sits within the resource-rich region historically held by the EOĂ — still one of the most plentiful and well-conditioned areas of the island today, which factored into its selection as the seat of the federal government.

Parliament
  • Formed a while after the EOĂ, replacing Imperial rule
  • Adopted a new flag — a brighter purple field than the muted shade used today, with a black cross and gold trim — marking a deliberate break from the Kingdom and Empire, while the gold trim carries forward the orange cross tradition of both prior flags and the black cross echoes the KOĂ flag's own black cross
  • Flag: View Flag
British Colonization & the Liberation War
  • During the era of British colonization, the British attempted to take Algrovik but succeeded in seizing only one area — present-day Evergreen
  • Evergreen remained under British occupation for 73 years
  • Throughout the occupation, Evergreen kept driving on the right — the British tried to make its citizens drive on the left, but because the national rule covered roads, rail, and waterways together, changing one mode alone caused too many complications and the attempt was abandoned (see Roads & Vehicles)
  • The British built no rail in Evergreen, making use of the very little existing track that was already there
  • 47 years into the occupation, the Algrovik government began planning a surprise attack to retake Evergreen
  • At the 73-year mark, the attack was launched: a combined ground and naval assault on government buildings, alongside raids on civilian buildings to hunt down remaining British officials
  • Algrovik won the war — known as the Liberation War — and the British were expelled
  • Upon its return, Evergreen was made a Special Administration District and integrated with the country's other districts, while retaining the distinct culture that British rule had left there
Federalization
  • Following the Liberation War, the country reformed into a federal system
  • This federal structure remains active to the present day
  • The flag was not changed
  • Coat of Arms: White field with a gold cross, echoing the national flag's cross motif. A black raven — a nod to the country's Old Norse cultural roots — perches at the center of the cross. A jeweled royal crown, inherited from the Kingdom of Ălgrøvík era, sits above the cross, and a red ribbon banner rests below
The Old Castle Museum
  • The original royal castle from the Kingdom era, located in Åbóvìk, Østháv — the site of the island's first settlement — has been preserved as a full federal museum
  • Operated and maintained by the federal government
  • Its rooms have been restored to look as they would have during the Kingdom era, offering visitors a walkthrough experience of the era rather than a static exhibit
  • Period displays include armor stands and the original KOĂ and EOĂ flags on the walls
  • Tours of the museum are free, but must be reserved ahead of time
  • Birthday reservations can also be made, allowing groups to book the museum for birthday celebrations
  • A live band, dressed in period clothing from the Kingdom era, performs historical songs from both the KOĂ and EOĂ eras for visitors
  • The Royal Guardsmen, a ceremonial legacy unit within the Ministry of Military Forces, stand guard at the museum in recreations of KOĂ-era king's-guard uniforms — see Royal Guardsmen (Kóngègàrdèn) under Defense & Military Forces for details
Technology Museum
  • A privately owned museum dedicated to the country's scientific and technological history, serving as a modern counterpart to the historically-focused Old Castle Museum
  • Covers exhibits such as the country's 2010 Digital Sovereignty transition, its renewable energy infrastructure (nuclear, geothermal, wind, and hydroelectric), and domestic technology achievements
  • Admission is paid, though kept low-cost to remain accessible

Folklore & Mythology
Náttfìskmànn ("Night Fishman")
  • A docile, humanoid species said to inhabit the waters surrounding Algrovik — green fish scales, a long shark-like tail, and finned arms and legs with webbed hands and feet
  • Fully nocturnal on land and strictly piscivorous, feeding only on fish
  • Docile and avoidant of humans when left alone
  • Only comes ashore at night, and only along undeveloped, rocky stretches of coastline far from any city or town — sightings are unheard of near populated areas like Great Bay Docks or Pine Ridge
  • Known informally in older coastal storytelling as the Fískmànnèn
  • Most associated with the remote, rugged coastline of the outer districts, particularly Fjørálùnd and Víkthåvèn
Digital Sovereignty (2010)
  • In 2010, under the Ministry of Sustainability, Self-sufficiency and Independence, the government moved all government computers off of Windows
  • The country adopted a custom, self-maintained Linux distribution based on Debian
  • Two versions of the distro exist — a government version with pre-installed government software ("Ălgrøvík GNU/Linux, Government Edition"), and a public version without it ("Ălgrøvík GNU/Linux, Public Edition")

Defense & Military Forces

Fórsvàrsstỳrkèr óg Mílìtærstỳrkèr

Overview

Algrovik splits its armed structure across two separate federal ministries rather than a single unified military — a division rooted in what each is meant to do.

Ministry of Defense Forces — Territorial and internal defense — border and coastal security, air defense, and protection of the homeland itself, with each branch also keeping a small disaster-support element that assists the National Fire and Rescue Service on request (for the Air Guard, a mass-evacuation unit). Its three branches mirror one another: the Air Guard defends the sky, the Homeland Guard defends the land, and the Coast Guard defends the water. None operates offensively or deploys abroad.

Ministry of Military Forces — National defense capability and offensive military operations across all branches — the force Algrovik would field in an armed conflict beyond its own borders, alongside broader national security and deterrence.

Despite the ministerial split, every recruit — Defense Forces and Military Forces alike — trains at the same institution: the Central Military College outside Víkí. Doctrine, drill standards, and baseline training are shared, even though day-to-day command and mission scope diverge sharply once a recruit is assigned to a branch.

Ministry of Defense Forces

Charged with keeping Algrovik's own borders, coastline, and population secure. None of its three branches is structured or equipped for offensive operations — all three exist to respond, patrol, and protect rather than to project force.

Coast Guard (Kýstvàkt)

Coastal and territorial-water defense — the Defense Forces branch that guards Algrovik's waters, as the Air Guard guards its sky and the Homeland Guard its land. It is a defensive force only: it does not enforce fishing law, police civilian waterways, or run rescue operations. Naval Forces Command is the separate deployable, blue-water force.

  • Primary base of operations is Great Bay Docks, alongside the commercial fishing fleets and shipping traffic it helps secure
  • Maintains a rotating patrol presence in Algrovik's coastal and territorial waters, especially around Great Bay Docks and the busier shipping lanes, to detect and intercept hostile or unidentified vessels
  • Provides harbor defense at Great Bay Docks, including anti-intrusion and mine countermeasures
  • Intercept and hand off: anything criminal that the Coast Guard encounters (smuggling, illegal entry) is handed to the National Police Service or the Ministry of Border and Customs rather than handled by the Coast Guard itself
  • Fishing violations: the Coast Guard does not board vessels for fishing violations. It observes and reports them to the NPS Game Wardens, who enforce fishing law at sea. It may still board for safety, security, or defense reasons
  • Rescue is not a main role. All search and rescue is run by the National Fire and Rescue Service. Like the Homeland Guard, the Coast Guard keeps only a small disaster-support element that deploys on NFRS request during large events (for example, a major sea disaster), adding ships, crews, and divers under NFRS direction. It is not a standing rescue service

Structure:

  • Fleet Command — patrol vessels and cutters responsible for coastal and territorial-water presence and interdiction
  • Patrol Division — small, fast inshore craft that provide coastal patrol and harbor approaches security
  • Port Security Detachment — stationed at Great Bay Docks, handles harbor defense and protection of the Coast Guard and Naval facilities there (vessel and cargo inspections at ports of entry belong to the Ministry of Border and Customs)
  • Disaster Support Element — a small unit of ships, crews, and divers that assists the NFRS on request during large maritime emergencies

Rank Ladder (W.I.P.):

  1. Seaman Recruit — Entry rank, fresh out of the Central Military College
  2. Seaman — Standard deckhand-level crew member
  3. Petty Officer — Junior non-commissioned lead, oversees a small crew or station
  4. Chief Petty Officer — Senior enlisted rank, runs day-to-day operations aboard a vessel
  5. Lieutenant — Junior officer, commands smaller patrol craft
  6. Commander — Commands a cutter or shore station
  7. Commodore — Oversees Fleet Command, the Patrol Division, the Port Security Detachment, or the Disaster Support Element as a whole
  8. Vice Admiral — Highest rank within the Coast Guard itself; commands the branch as a whole, reporting up to the Ministry of Defense Forces leadership
Homeland Guard (Héìmèvàkt)

Internal defense, disaster support, and protection of critical infrastructure within Algrovik's own borders.

  • Organized into 5 District Guard Companies, one per district, each capable of mobilizing independently within its own district
  • Supports NFRS-led disaster response during large-scale disasters — wildfires, major flooding, and other events beyond NFRS and NEMS's standing capacity — by supplying additional manpower and equipment
  • Guards critical infrastructure such as power plants, dams, and government buildings, working alongside NPS Peace Officers during major public events
  • Composed largely of part-time reservists who train regularly but hold civilian jobs, rather than a standing full-time force

Structure:

  • District Guard Companies (×5) — one per district, each with its own local commander reporting up to Homeland Guard headquarters
  • Infrastructure Protection Detail — standing guard rotations at power plants, dams, and government buildings
  • Disaster Response Element — mobilized on request from NFRS or NEMS during large-scale emergencies

Rank Ladder (W.I.P.):

  1. Guardsman — Entry rank for part-time reservists
  2. Senior Guardsman — Experienced reservist, may lead a small detail
  3. Guard Sergeant — Non-commissioned lead of a squad-sized element
  4. Guard Lieutenant — Junior officer within a District Guard Company
  5. Company Commander — Commands one of the 5 District Guard Companies
  6. Guard General — Highest rank within the Homeland Guard itself; commands the branch as a whole, reporting up to the Ministry of Defense Forces leadership
Air Guard (Lúftvàkt)

Ground-based air defense — protecting Algrovik's airspace over key strategic points nationwide. Distinct from the Ministry of Military Forces' Air Forces Command, which handles offensive/deployable air power — the Air Guard is purely defensive and does not operate aircraft of its own.

  • Operates a nationwide network of ground-based anti-missile and air-defense systems, centralized around the country's most strategically significant locations rather than spread evenly across all 5 districts
  • Primary coverage points include Víkí (federal capital), Great Bay Docks (major port and economic hub), and the Central Military College
  • Works in coordination with NORDEFCO partner nations on shared radar and early-warning systems
  • Like the Coast Guard and Homeland Guard, keeps a small disaster-support element. Because the Air Guard operates no aircraft, its role is mass evacuation on NFRS request rather than rescue or airlift
  • Composed of a mix of full-time personnel and part-time reservists, given the specialized, standing-watch nature of air-defense monitoring compared to the Homeland Guard's more reservist-heavy model

Structure:

  • Air Defense Detachments — stationed at key strategic points nationwide, each operating and maintaining its own anti-missile and air-defense systems
  • Early Warning & Radar Element — monitors national airspace and coordinates with NORDEFCO partner nations on shared radar coverage
  • Mass Evacuation Element — a small unit that, on NFRS request, organizes and carries out large-scale evacuations of civilians during major emergencies: setting up assembly points, marshalling and routing convoys, and coordinating buses, trains, and other transport. It works under NFRS direction and is not a standing rescue service

Rank Ladder (W.I.P.):

  1. Guardsman — Entry rank, standard air-defense systems operator
  2. Senior Guardsman — Experienced systems operator, may train new recruits
  3. Guard Sergeant — Non-commissioned lead of a detachment's operating crew
  4. Guard Lieutenant — Junior officer, leads a section within an Air Defense Detachment
  5. Detachment Commander — Commands one of the Air Defense Detachments, the Early Warning & Radar Element, or the Mass Evacuation Element
  6. Guard General — Highest rank within the Air Guard itself; commands the branch as a whole, reporting up to the Ministry of Defense Forces leadership

Naval Forces Command (Sjøfórsvàrèt)

  • Represents Algrovik's blue-water and deployable naval capability, as opposed to the Coast Guard's coastal and territorial-water defense role

Legacy & Ceremonial Unit: Royal Guardsmen (Kóngègàrdèn)

A legacy unit tracing back to the King's Guard of the Kingdom of Ălgrøvík (KOĂ) era — retained today as a purely ceremonial company rather than an operational fighting force.

  • Originally formed to protect the monarch during the KOĂ era, the unit survived the Empire, Parliament, and Federalization eras by shedding its operational role and continuing on in a ceremonial capacity
  • Guardsmen wear faithful recreations of KOĂ-era king's-guard suits and uniforms — styled in the Kingdom's historic blue, black, and orange, rather than the modern purple-and-gold of the current national colors
  • Stands ceremonial guard at the Old Castle Museum in Åbóvìk, Østháv, alongside the museum's period-dressed live band, giving visitors a consistent walkthrough experience of the Kingdom era
  • Serves as the honor guard for state functions — presidential inaugurations, official visits, and swearing-in ceremonies — and marches in the Liberation Day and Settlers Day observances
  • Does not carry live weapons or participate in operational duty; ceremonial arms (pikes, halberds, and dress sabres) are carried purely for pageantry
  • Membership is drawn from soldiers already serving elsewhere in the Military Forces who volunteer and are selected for ceremonial duty, rather than through a separate recruiting pipeline

Structure:

  • Guard Company — a single standing company headquartered in Víkí, sized to rotate ceremonial postings between the Old Castle Museum in Åbóvìk and state functions nationwide

Rank Ladder (W.I.P.):

  1. Guardsman — Entry rank within the ceremonial company
  2. Guard Corporal — Leads a small file of Guardsmen during postings and processions
  3. Guard Sergeant — Senior non-commissioned rank, oversees drill standards and uniform inspection
  4. Guard Lieutenant — Junior officer, plans ceremonial postings and event coordination
  5. Captain of the Guard — Commands the Guard Company as a whole
Central Military College

Located near the federal capital, Víkí, the Central Military College is where every recruit trains, regardless of which command or branch they'll ultimately serve under. Made up of three buildings:

  • Main Training Building — Divided into 3 sections, one for each Military Forces command — Air, Ground, and Naval
  • Defense Forces Building — Divided into 3 sections, one for each Defense Forces branch — Coast Guard, Homeland Guard, and Air Guard
  • Instructor Building — Housing and offices for military instructors, staffed under the Training and Military Doctrine Command

Alongside baseline recruit training, the College issues its own trade certificates in fields with civilian equivalents — e.g. an advanced vehicle mechanic certificate — which are recognized by civilian employers and can be used to pursue related civilian jobs after service.

NPS Military Police recruits also complete a training track at the Central Military College, training alongside actual Military and Defense Forces recruits — the only NPS sub-unit with this requirement. They remain NPS personnel throughout and never fall under either ministry's chain of command. See the National Emergency Services section for details.

Foreign Military Presence

Having endured 73 years of British occupation in Evergreen S.A.D., ended only by the Liberation War, Algrovik's modern defense policy draws a firm distinction between cooperative support presence and combatant military presence. The country welcomes allied support personnel — logistics, technical, radar, medical — as mutually beneficial security cooperation consistent with its NORDEFCO membership, but does not permit foreign combatant forces (infantry, strike aircraft, offensive naval vessels) to be stationed on its soil. This policy is rooted directly in the national memory of the Liberation War and the decades of occupation that preceded it — even among trusted allies, Algrovik remains firmly protective of sole combat sovereignty over its own territory.

The Right-Hand Rule
  • The Right-Hand Rule: Algrovik's oldest traffic rule, first set for carts, was written to cover "all traffic of any kind" — so every vehicle on road, rail, or inland waterway keeps to the right, in all five districts
  • Rail: Before the British occupation, the country already had a small amount of rail — rare, cargo-only, and single track shared by both directions, with small sidings where trains passed one another. The rule applied there: trains meeting at a siding each took the right-hand track. Today, Trains and Rail Buses operate on the right-hand side by law, matching road traffic
  • Inland waterways: On narrow inland waterways leading to small inland docks, vessels keep to the right of the channel and pass other vessels port to port. NPS Traffic Enforcement's small-craft element enforces this, and the Ministry of Transportation sets the vessel rules

Note — Color Particles: Introduced during the transition from Old Ăllgrøvíàn to Ăllgrøvíàn. Two 2-letter particles attach to the front of a color word to indicate shade. Kø- denotes a dark shade, and Rý- denotes a light shade; a color word with no particle is its "normal" shade. Kø and Rý also function as standalone color words in their own right — Kø alone means black, and Rý alone means white. Combined with each other rather than with another color, Kø-Rý means dark gray and Rý-Kø means light gray (the first element is dominant, the second modifies it toward its shade).

Note — Mood Particles: Also introduced during the transition from Old Ăllgrøvíàn to Ăllgrøvíàn. A written-only feature with no spoken equivalent — mood particles exist purely on the page and are not pronounced or spoken aloud. A small set of 2-letter particles attach to the front of a sentence, followed by a hyphen, in the form (Mood)-(Sentence) — e.g. Sø-Héìmăvăll ér fágùr. or Vá-Héìmăvăll ér fágùr! — marking the emotional tone the sentence should be read in (e.g. grief, joy, anger, tenderness, fear, solemnity, humor). The particle governs that sentence only, up through its terminal punctuation (. ! ?), and stacks with punctuation rather than replacing it — a sentence can still be a question or exclamation while also carrying a mood particle. Every new sentence must either carry its own mood particle or explicitly carry none; a mood is never carried over by default from the previous sentence. Legal and government documents are written without mood particles by default, with one exception: direct quotation of spoken testimony or statements (e.g. witness testimony, defendant statements, courtroom dialogue), where the speaker's mood particle is preserved as part of the accurate record.

Note — Vowel Length: A written-only distinction introduced during the transition from Old Norse to Old Ăllgrøvíàn, marking a vowel-length contrast Old Norse itself carried but which faded out of most of its modern descendants. Every vowel that historically carried an acute accent (A, E, I, O, U, Y) now has a grave-accented counterpart (À, È, Ì, Ò, Ù, Ỳ) standing in for the old plain, unmarked letter — so no vowel in this set appears bare anymore. The acute form marks the long vowel and is pronounced accordingly, drawn out longer than its plain counterpart. The grave form, however, changes nothing about the sound — it is pronounced exactly like the ordinary, unaccented letter always was; the mark exists purely to visually confirm "this vowel is short" rather than to indicate any actual shift in pronunciation. In effect, only the acute accent is phonetic — the grave accent is cosmetic, a scribal convention carried over from how the Old Norse-to-Old-Ăllgrøvíàn transition formalized short vowels in writing once long vowels started being marked. Because the two accents lean in opposite directions — grave tilting back, acute tilting forward — a reader can still tell short from long at a glance, even though only one of the two marks reflects a real difference in speech. Length is lexical rather than positional — fixed to each word as a whole, and not carried over into the compounds and derived forms built from it, rather than shifting based on where the syllable falls in the word. This is why a shared element like "-vík" / "-vìk" (bay) surfaces as long in some place names (Ălgrøvík, Börgvík) but short in others (Åbóvìk, Éspvìk) — each name's length is simply part of that particular word's identity, the way stress or gender might be fixed to a word in other languages, rather than derived from any surrounding rule.

  • The most common work/school week runs Monday, Tuesday, Thursday, and Friday as full days

Overseen by the Ministry of Education, administering the Free Education Act nationwide.

  • Middle Level School — Grades 7–9

Working Students
  • Students aged 16 or older who are employed may have their work take priority over school, with non-essential subjects — such as art and gym — excused
  • Core subjects, including math and history, remain mandatory regardless of work status
  • To qualify for excusal, a student must work at least 22 hours per week, across at least 4 days per week
  • This applies to working students at any grade level, not just Higher Level School
  • Verification is handled by the student's employer, who submits proof of the student's work schedule directly to the school board — this paperwork must be resubmitted every 3 months to keep the excusal active

  • The other four districts: Schools teach in Ăllgrøvíàn, with an English class
School Year & Daily Schedule
  • Breaks: The school year has two main breaks, a summer break and a winter break
  • Structure: Every school day has the same shape — a morning block, a 1-hour midday break, and an afternoon block — with a few 15-minute breaks between classes. Only the length of the blocks changes by level
  • Higher Level School (Grades 10–12): 4 hours in the morning, a 1-hour break (students may leave school during it), then 4 more hours — a full 8-hour class day that mirrors a standard work day
  • Middle Level School (Grades 7–9): Shorter blocks of 3 hours each, for 6 class hours a day
  • Lower Level School (Grades 1–6): Shorter still, with blocks of 2 hours each, for 4 class hours a day
  • Philosophy: Classes are relaxed rather than "super strict," but the system is deliberately built to emulate real working hours, so that high school students are mentally prepared for the workplace (see also Working Students)
  • Wednesdays: As a half school day, only the morning block runs at every level (see Work Week)

District Area Codes
  • Phone numbers follow the format +10-DCC-XXX-XXXX, where the first digit is the district ID (see Districts), the following 2 digits are the city code, and the remaining 7 digits are freely assigned to subscribers with no further block reservations (e.g. +10-101-535-4512 is district 1 (Evergreen S.A.D.), city 01 (Burlington), followed by a random subscriber number)
  • District and city codes are assigned alphabetically — the lowest code goes to whichever district (or, within a district, whichever city) comes first alphabetically, with each subsequent one taking the next code up, and city codes start at 01 within each district
  • Evergreen S.A.D.: 1
  • Fjørálùnd: 2
  • Østháv: 3
  • Skjærmørk: 4
  • Víkthåvèn: 5
  • Within Evergreen S.A.D., city codes are assigned alphabetically: Burlington = 01 (prefix 101), Cambridge = 02 (prefix 102), Danvers = 03 (prefix 103), Pine Ridge = 04 (prefix 104)
  • Because the district digit and city digits together form a single combined prefix, every district-plus-city combination is inherently unique nationwide — there's no code-sharing between districts under this system
  • Each district can support up to 99 cities (01–99), and each individual city prefix has 10,000,000 possible subscriber numbers (0000000–9999999) — far beyond what any city in Algrovik would need, leaving enormous headroom for growth without ever requiring renumbering
  • 000: Reserved for government use, and doubles as the national emergency line
    • 000 alone reaches a standard dispatch center
    • 000-1 connects directly to Police (NPS)
    • 000-2 connects directly to Fire and Rescue (NFRS)
    • 000-3 connects directly to EMS (NEMS)
  • 010: The country's equivalent of an 800/toll-free number in the U.S. — covers any line operated by a company or business as a whole. Any registered company or business can apply for a 010 number free of charge with valid proof of legal business documentation
  • 001: Reserved for pagers, which remain in common use throughout the country
  • The special codes 000, 001, and 010 all start with the digit 0, which is never used as a district code (districts run 1–9), so they can never collide with a real district-plus-city prefix
  • The system is designed less around raw numbering capacity and more around fast identification — seeing a number's district and city prefix immediately tells you which district and city a call originates from, or whether it's a government line, a business hotline, or a pager, before the call is ever answered
Pagers
  • Pagers remain widely used in modern-day Algrovik, not just by emergency workers but by much of the general public
  • Most citizens also own smartphones or have landlines — pagers are used alongside these rather than replacing them
  • The government runs a free program that provides citizens with a pager and a pager number at no cost
  • Pagers issued through the free program can only send and receive text messages, but come with unlimited data for texting
Housing
  • The majority of the population lives in apartments rather than standalone houses
  • Apartment units are large by typical standards — each unit is roughly the size of a small house
  • This approach allows housing to be compacted into smaller footprints, letting population density remain high in developed areas while preserving the country's large forests and other stretches of land unsuitable for building
Retail & Commercial Space
  • The same space-saving approach used in housing carries over to retail — most stores are consolidated into multi-story malls rather than standing alone, built upward for the same reason apartments are: preserving land rather than spreading development outward
  • Malls are typically a few stories tall, housing a mix of smaller shops, restaurants, and services under one roof
  • Standalone stores and dedicated single-purpose buildings still exist, particularly for larger businesses or those requiring their own dedicated space, but they're the exception rather than the norm in more developed areas
  • Standalone houses follow the same pattern as standalone stores — both exist, but are considerably less common than their compacted counterparts (apartments and malls, respectively)
  • This pattern is most pronounced in denser, more urban areas — semi-rural stretches like Jackson Ave in Pine Ridge still see standalone buildings, farmhouses, and undeveloped land more often
Standards & Formats
  • Time Format: 24-hour — reflects the country's generally practical, ambiguity-free approach to systems (similar to the phone numbering plan's design), reinforced by mandatory National Security Act service, where conscripts learn 24-hour time as standard and carry the habit into civilian life afterward
  • Time Zone: UTC+0 all year, with no daylight saving — the island sits close to 0° longitude, so clock time matches solar time closely, and with such long summer days and short winter days a seasonal clock change would add little (as in Iceland). The same time applies in all five districts, including Evergreen
  • Date Format: Day-Month-Year — the natural convention inherited from the country's Norwegian and Icelandic cultural roots, later locked in as the official government standard during the 2010 Digital Sovereignty push
  • Temperature Format: Celsius — consistent with the country's broader Nordic/European alignment and NORDEFCO membership; also practically useful given how central freezing and near-freezing conditions are to daily life and weather
  • Measurement System: Metric — inherited from the country's Norwegian/Icelandic roots, and reinforced by the same heavy STEM emphasis in schools that produced the scientific notation convention below
  • Week: The week starts on Monday, and weeks are numbered through the year — in keeping with the country's practical, ambiguity-free approach to systems
  • Decimal Separator: A comma (e.g., 12,50), used in all five districts including Evergreen S.A.D., where language and currency are the only differences. (This reference document writes numbers in English style for readability)
  • Paper Size: A4, standard nationwide
  • Postal Codes: The district letters used on vehicle plates (EN, FD, ØV, SK, VN) followed by three digits, e.g., EN 482
  • Number Format: Scientific notation is the most common format used for general numbers — practical, transactional figures such as prices, phone numbers, addresses, and similar everyday identifiers are written normally rather than in scientific notation. This convention traces back to how schools historically taught mathematics nationwide, with heavy early emphasis on scientific notation that carried into everyday habit and eventually became the cultural default
Geography (W.I.P.)
Location
  • Algrovik sits in the Norwegian Sea/Greenland Sea, in the North Atlantic — positioned roughly between Iceland and Norway, north of Iceland and southwest of Svalbard
Area & Size
  • Approximately 130,500 km² (50,400 sq mi) — slightly larger than Iceland's real-world area of roughly 103,000 km²
Terrain
  • The country's terrain is very hilly overall
  • The outer edges of the island are largely covered in snowy tundra — flat, sparse, wind-swept grassland with patches of dry brush poking up through the snow cover, stretching out toward the horizon
Climate
  • Cool, wet, and changeable year-round — mild by northern-latitude standards near the coast due to moderating ocean currents, colder and snowier inland and toward the tundra edges
  • Rain is frequent along the coastal lowlands and in Evergreen S.A.D., especially from fall through spring
  • Snowfall is frequent in the hilly interior and tundra regions, heaviest and longest-lasting around the Mountain of Fjørálùnd
  • Overcast, drizzly weather is common even outside of storms
  • Summers are short and mild — cool rather than hot, with regular rain
  • Winters are long, with heavy snow inland and toward the tundra, and a colder wet-to-sleet mix along the coast
  • Weather can change noticeably within a single day, particularly near the coast
  • Fog is common around Great Bay Docks and other coastal areas, especially in the mornings
  • Wind is a near-constant presence across much of the country
  • The aurora is visible in the northern and tundra regions during winter

Mining
  • Iron — one of the country's largest mining industries, drawn from the hilly interior
  • Copper — one of the country's largest mining industries, often extracted alongside iron deposits
  • Titanium — one of the country's largest mining industries
  • Silver — mined on a smaller scale than iron, copper, or titanium; mining companies pay a 20% resource tax in-kind, handing over that share of mined silver directly rather than in cash, which the Ministry of Banking and Treasury holds in reserve as a form of physical-value backing
  • Gold — mined on a smaller scale than iron, copper, or titanium; mining companies pay a 20% resource tax in-kind, handing over that share of mined gold directly rather than in cash, which the Ministry of Banking and Treasury holds in reserve as a form of physical-value backing

Farming

Farming — both crops and livestock — is another major industry, shaped by the country's cool, wet climate and hilly terrain.

  • Crops: Cold-hardy staples including root vegetables (potatoes, turnips, carrots), barley, rye, oats, and cabbage-family vegetables, alongside apple orchards and Fróstdrùè grape cultivation used in producing Gúllfàll
  • Greenhouse Farming: Geothermal-heated greenhouses allow domestic cultivation of produce that wouldn't otherwise survive the climate, such as tomatoes and peppers
  • Livestock: Sheep and cattle are the primary livestock, suited to grazing the hilly terrain; dairy cattle support the country's dairy industry
  • Farming is concentrated most heavily in the hilly, inland districts of Fjørálùnd and Víkthåvèn, with lighter farming activity spread across Østháv and Skjærmørk; Evergreen S.A.D. sees comparatively little farming given its more urbanized, British-influenced development
  • Overseen by the Ministry of Agriculture, working alongside the Ministry of Sustainability, Self-sufficiency and Independence on agricultural self-sufficiency policy

Note: Greenhouse farming is used extensively rather than sparingly, allowing the country to grow a wide variety of produce that wouldn't naturally survive its climate, powered largely by the country's geothermal energy investment. Similarly, a range of farm animal breeds are imported and raised domestically alongside the native cold-hardy sheep and cattle, giving the country a wider variety of meats and dairy than its climate would otherwise support on its own.

Timber
  • Mørkétrè ("dark wood") — a native hardwood tree found throughout the country's forested interior, prized for its very dark brown coloring and unusual strength and density
  • Its density is largely attributed to the tree's slow growth in Algrovik's cold, harsh, wind-battered climate, producing unusually tight growth rings compared to hardwoods grown in milder conditions
  • Exported abroad as a specialty hardwood, and used domestically in shipbuilding, high-end furniture, and construction framing
  • Yellow Birch (Gúlbjørk) — a native tree with a pale, whitish-yellow wood, notably less strong than Mørkétrè; used mostly for decorative purposes such as furniture accents, veneer, and carved goods rather than structural work
  • Its sap is tapped and boiled down into syrup, produced domestically for local consumption with a portion exported abroad

EU Trade Arrangement
  • Algrovik is not an EU member, but takes part in the European single market through an EEA/EFTA-style arrangement, much like Iceland and Norway

  • The country stays outside the EU's common agricultural and fisheries policies, so it sets its own fishing quotas and farming support — important given that seafood is one of its major exports

Food & Drink
Skúggbær (Native Berry)
  • A small, dark purple-to-black berry native to Algrovik, growing wild across the hilly, forested interior — thriving in the same cool, damp, overcast conditions the country's climate is known for
  • Tart and slightly bitter when eaten raw, so it's rarely consumed fresh — its primary use is in preserving, fermenting, and distilling
  • Traditionally hand-foraged rather than farmed, in keeping with the country's self-sufficiency ethos
Gúllfàll (National Spirit)

A distilled spirit made from three ingredients: apples, Skúggbær, and a cold-hardy grape varietal known as Fróstdrùè — the latter bred and cultivated specifically to withstand Algrovik's cool, hilly, short-summer climate.

  • Ratio: 50% apple, 30% Skúggbær, 20% Fróstdrùè grape
  • Apple forms the smooth, familiar backbone of the spirit, reflecting its abundance domestically
  • Skúggbær lends the spirit its dark amber-to-violet color and distinct tartness — the ingredient that gives Gúllfàll its native, unmistakably Algrovik character
  • Fróstdrùè grapes make up the smallest share of the blend, used as an accent for aroma and complexity rather than bulk — a reflection of their lower yield and cold-hardy rarity
  • Generally treated as a refined, special-occasion spirit rather than an everyday pour, served at weddings, state functions, and national holidays such as Liberation Day
Skúmstàng (Dessert)
  • A soft bread stick baked around a light, airy foam center, meant to be torn apart and dipped rather than eaten whole
  • Often served plain or lightly dusted with sugar, alongside a small side of jam for dipping
  • No single "correct" jam accompanies it — households and regions typically use whatever's in season, with berry jams (particularly Skúggbær) being a common choice
  • A homey, informal dessert, typically served at family gatherings or alongside coffee rather than as a formal restaurant plating
